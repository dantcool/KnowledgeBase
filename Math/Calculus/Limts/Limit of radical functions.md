rational functions are just fractions divided by another fractions 
or includes square roots 

start the same as other limits by doing direct substitution but if you get 0/0 or even just a zero in the denominator thats when you put sum work in the formula 

Example 
$lim_{x->1}\frac{1/x-1/1}{x-1}$

we multiply by the denominators of the top part of the fraction which is 1x or just x 

1/x*1x/x is just 1 then 1/1x*1/1 is just x making the top of our equation

$=\frac{1-x}{(x-1)(1x)}$

we didnt multiply out the 1x to the bottom just left it as is now notice 1-x and x-1 are almost identical we just factor out the negative from the top giving us 

$\frac{-1(x-1)}{(x-1)(1x)}$

the x-1 cancels out then we can plug in our x again giving us -1/1 which is ultimately just 
=-1

EXAMPLE

$lim_{x->16}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}-4 } - \frac{8}{x-16})$

Trying direct substitution gives us a zero in our denominator so we gotta try something else 

like combining our fraction into one and if you notice we can use the difference of squares factoring rule 
$(a^2-b^2 = (a-b)(a+b))$
$(\sqrt{x})^2=x$ and  $4^2=16$
$x-16 = (\sqrt{x}-4)(\sqrt{x}+4)$

then we creat a common denominator by multiplying our first fraction thats was missing the $\sqrt{x}+4$ so we multiply it to our frist fraction top and bottom to give us our missing piece 

$\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}-4} * \frac{\sqrt{x}+4}{\sqrt{x}+4} - \frac{8}{(\sqrt{x}-4)(\sqrt{x}+4)}$

now we can combine and simplify 

$\frac{\sqrt{x}-4}{(\sqrt{x}-4)(\sqrt{x}+4)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}+4}$

now we can direct substitute and we get 1/8
